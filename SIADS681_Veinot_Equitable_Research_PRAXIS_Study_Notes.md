# Equitable Research PRAXIS

*Veinot et al. (2022), Yearbook of Medical Informatics, pp. 307–316.* [Article DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0042-1742542)

## Key points

- **Main argument.** Health informatics researchers should build equity into decisions about what to study, who participates, what gets measured, and how results are interpreted. A digital health tool can widen disparities if the people facing the largest barriers have the least access to it or benefit from it less.
- The authors synthesize a literature review and lessons from **nine equity-focused studies in the United States and Canada**. The studies illustrate how to apply the framework at four stages of research.
- Representation involves whose experiences are captured in the data, whose interpretation shapes the findings, and who has real influence over decisions.

## The four parts of PRAXIS

| Component | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Participation and Representation** | Share decision-making power with marginalized communities, include their perspectives in the data and interpretation, and represent their strengths and resources alongside the barriers they face. |
| **Appropriate Methods and Interventions** | Adapt recruitment, measurement, and technology to the population’s language, accessibility needs, and actual technology access. |
| **ConteXtualization and Structural Competence** | Examine how conditions such as racism, broadband access, transportation, and institutional practices shape health and technology use. Choose a level of intervention that can address the barrier. |
| **Investigation of Systematic Differences** | Plan to measure how access, use, outcomes, or model errors vary across groups and settings. Examine whether the intervention itself could widen an existing gap. |

## 1. Project initiation

- Give community partners meaningful influence over questions and outcomes. **Community-based participatory research (CBPR)** involves community members throughout the project. **Transformative mixed methods** combine methods within a social justice framework, with attention to community capacity. **Action research** uses repeated cycles of looking, thinking, and acting with stakeholders.
- **Shared Mobility example.** A Detroit federally qualified health center identified missed appointments and transportation barriers as priorities. The team co-developed a technology-enabled ride-sharing pilot and made missed appointments an evaluation outcome.
- **Blood pressure example.** Some government-issued phones blocked short-code text messages. The follow-on MI-BP project moved to an app. The example shows why owning a phone and having access to a specific service are separate measurements.

## 2. Sampling and recruitment

- Assess who can realistically be reached by each recruitment channel. Patient portal recruitment can favor people who already use portals. The blood pressure studies recruited in emergency departments where members of the target population already sought care.
- Plan sample size around the comparisons you want to make. **Oversampling** recruits or selects more observations from groups that would otherwise have too few for meaningful analysis. The Neighborhood Effects study oversampled health-related tweets from disadvantaged census tracts. Qualitative studies can plan for **saturation** within subgroups, when additional interviews yield little new relevant information.

## 3. Data collection

- Use accessible instruments that let people describe themselves. For example, sexual orientation can involve identity, behavior, and attraction, and those dimensions can require different questions.
- Check how a secondary data source was created. In the Telehealth Uptake study, insurance data were tied to billed encounters, leaving gaps when the researchers tried to use insurance as a patient-level proxy for socioeconomic status. The PCORNet example shows how grouping American Sign Language under “other” can make Deaf patients hard to identify for analysis.
- Observe tools in the settings where people will use them. During Dialysafe usability testing, patients tried a tablet while receiving dialysis. Limits on movement and device positioning surfaced in that setting.

## 4. Data analysis

- Invite community partners to help code qualitative data and review interpretations. **Reflexivity** means documenting and examining how researchers’ assumptions and positions influence what they observe and conclude.
- Match the analysis to the context. Multilevel models can account for patients clustered within clinics or neighborhoods. **Stratified analysis** estimates results separately for subgroups. **Moderation analysis** tests whether a relationship or intervention effect differs across groups or conditions.
- Audit model performance across relevant groups. The Neighborhood Effects team evaluated a tweet classifier using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 across groups characterized with census-tract data. After identifying representation issues, they collected and labeled more tweets from tracts with more marginalized residents and retrained the model.

## Terms to remember

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Asset-based representation** | Describing community knowledge, strengths, and resources as part of the research. |
| **Structural competence** | Recognizing how policies, institutions, and material conditions produce inequities and using that understanding to frame questions and interventions. |
| **Intervention-generated inequality** | An intervention widens a gap when access, uptake, or benefit differs systematically across groups. |
| **Individual and group fairness** | Individual fairness asks whether similar individuals are treated similarly. Group fairness examines whether relevant model outcomes or performance measures are comparable across groups, according to the fairness criterion used. |

## Conclusion

PRAXIS makes equity a research design responsibility from project initiation through analysis. Community participation can change the study question, the recruitment strategy, and the interpretation of findings. The authors also emphasize participatory dissemination, although their detailed guidance focuses on the four research stages above.
