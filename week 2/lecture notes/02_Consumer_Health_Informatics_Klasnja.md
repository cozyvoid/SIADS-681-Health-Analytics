# Module 2 — Consumer Health Informatics: Designing Health Technologies for Everyday Life

**Lecturer:** Pedja Klasnja  
**Source:** *Week 2 - Consumer Health Informatics - Klasnja*, 37 slides.

## Key points
1. Consumer health informatics (CHI) designs and evaluates technologies that support people's everyday health management, beyond clinical visits.
2. Examples include patient portals, self-tracking, behavior-change apps, chronic-condition support, mental-health tools, and remote monitoring.
3. Good designs balance the benefit of information against the burden of collecting, interpreting, and acting on it.
4. Adaptation, integration into routines, social interactions, and privacy are major design considerations.
5. Personalized and just-in-time adaptive interventions attempt to make support more relevant to an individual's changing circumstances.

## 1. Purpose and definition (slides 2–5)
A case describes **John**, age 53, who previously had a heart attack and has hypertension. He wants to reduce cardiovascular risk, potentially with mobile and wearable tools.

**Consumer health informatics (CHI):** Design and evaluation of technologies supporting individuals as they manage their health in day-to-day life.

The lecture quotes a definition emphasizing systems that (a) directly serve consumers, (b) receive personal information and offer personalized output, and (c) can be used with clinicians but do not depend on clinician operation.

**Distinction:** CHI emphasizes day-to-day decisions and experiences, not solely clinician-facing electronic health records.

## 2. Managing information (slides 6–10)
- **Personal health records (PHRs):** Consumer-managed health-information collections; the slides show a former HealthVault product.
- **Patient portals:** Interfaces for appointments, records, medications, and communication with a healthcare organization.
- **Non-clinical personal information:** Symptom logs, tracking diaries, daily check-ins, and other observations that may not appear in routine clinical records.

**Example:** A patient portal communicates lab results, whereas a personal log might record when symptoms occurred between visits.

## 3. Supporting health behaviors (slides 11–21)
Technologies can encourage healthy habits and help with activity planning, chronic diabetes management, games for education/motivation, mental-health exercises, and behaviors such as smoking cessation, healthy eating, glucose monitoring, visit attendance, and medication use.

**Examples shown:** activity-tracking applications, the MAHI diabetes-management research system, the BlueStar diabetes-support application, mobile learning games, and mindfulness or anxiety-support applications.

**Takeaway:** A tool must support *behavior and reflection*, not merely display numbers.

## 4. Symptoms, status, and remote monitoring (slides 22–24)
**Remote monitoring:** Collecting health measurements outside the clinic and transmitting them for review or feedback. A slide illustrates home measures such as blood pressure, heart rate, medication, and weight flowing to a monitoring center and clinician; another shows mobile ECG data.

**Example:** Repeated home measurements can reveal a pattern obscured by a single clinic measurement.

## 5. Design considerations (slides 25–32)
- **Value versus burden:** Does the benefit justify data entry, notifications, maintenance, and attention demands? A slide invokes an older Google Health interface.
- **Adaptation:** Change features or feedback as user circumstances or progress change.
- **Integration with daily life:** Fit routines rather than forcing users into impractical workflows.
- **Social interactions:** Social support can be built into mobile tools, but not every user wants sharing.
- **Privacy concerns:** Sensitive behavioral data may be useful to insurers or others in ways users did not intend; a slide specifically raises fitness-tracking and life insurance.

**Example:** A nutrition tracker that requires laborious food searches may lose users despite offering detailed dietary data.

## 6. Personalized and just-in-time interventions (slides 33–37)
**JITAI (just-in-time adaptive intervention):** An intervention that continuously adapts its operation to improve health and user-experience outcomes (lecture's definition, slide 35).

**Example:** Offering activity support when it is contextually useful, rather than sending the same reminder at every hour. This is an illustrative example of the concept, not a reported trial result from the slide.

The lecture explicitly raises the question of **when JITAIs are inappropriate**: prompts can be mistimed, intrusive, burdensome, or unsafe depending on the context. Consumer health technologies can also help researchers understand behavior over time (slide 37).

## Important terms
- **CHI:** Consumer health informatics.
- **PHR:** Personal health record, often maintained for the individual.
- **Patient portal:** Organization-connected interface to healthcare services and records.
- **Self-monitoring:** Tracking one's own behavior, symptoms, or physiological status.
- **Remote monitoring:** Health observations collected outside a clinic and shared for monitoring.
- **JITAI:** A context-sensitive intervention that adapts over time.

## Main argument / conclusion
**A useful consumer health technology must account for lived experience: user needs, routines, burden, adaptation, privacy, and the appropriate timing of support matter as much as its technical capabilities.**
