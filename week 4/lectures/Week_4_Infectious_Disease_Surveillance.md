# Module 4 (Week 4): Infectious Disease Surveillance — Strategies and Examples

## Lecture at a Glance

This lecture explains how infectious-disease surveillance developed in the United States, how surveillance systems are designed, and how multiple national systems are used to monitor disease, vaccination, antimicrobial resistance, and emerging infections.

The central theme is that surveillance is not a single database. It is a network of systems with different purposes, populations, reporting mechanisms, and biases. Good public-health interpretation requires knowing **what population a surveillance system captures, how cases are defined, and how the data were generated**.

---

## Key Points

1. Infectious-disease surveillance in the U.S. has evolved from local disease reporting to large national systems.
2. U.S. public-health responsibility is distributed across state, local, and federal governments, creating fragmentation.
3. **Active** and **passive** surveillance differ in how cases are identified and therefore have different strengths and biases.
4. Surveillance performance depends heavily on the **case definition**, testing strategy, and clinical presentation of infection.
5. The “iceberg” concept shows that observed clinical cases may represent only a fraction of all infections.
6. Major U.S. surveillance systems monitor notifiable diseases, foodborne illness, antimicrobial resistance, vaccination, adverse events, and respiratory viruses.
7. Surveillance data can directly affect clinical and public-health policy.
8. Existing systems can be adapted to detect emerging diseases, as demonstrated during COVID-19.

# Part 1. History of Infectious-Disease Surveillance

## Early U.S. surveillance

Selected milestones from the lecture:

- **1741:** Rhode Island legislation required tavern keepers to report smallpox, yellow fever, and cholera.
- **1850:** Census-based surveillance.
- **1878:** Congress authorized the U.S. Public Health Service to collect information on quarantinable diseases such as cholera, plague, smallpox, and yellow fever.
- **Post-WWII:** CDC developed from efforts to conduct malaria surveillance in military bases and returning veterans through **Malaria Control in War Areas (MCWA)**.
- After a major **1907 polio outbreak in New York City**, poliovirus surveillance became a routine Public Health Service function.
- **1957:** Polio vaccination coverage began to be monitored by the U.S. Census.
- **1960:** The **Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (MMWR)** was established.

These examples show surveillance expanding from local outbreak reporting into national, routine monitoring.

# 2. A Structural Challenge: U.S. Public Health Is Fragmented

A major theme is the division of responsibility between state/local and federal governments.

## State and local roles

Examples include:

- Monitoring disease burden
- Surveillance of notifiable diseases
- Implementing public-health regulations
- Providing services such as vaccination or needle-exchange programs

## Federal roles

Examples include:

- Policymaking
- Financing
- Public-health protection
- Collecting and disseminating national health information
- Building population-health capacity
- Direct management of some services

### Why fragmentation matters

The lecture notes that:

- State law governs much of public health.
- Dedicated public-health funding differs by state.
- Some state laws are over 100 years old and reflect older understandings of infectious disease.
- The overall system is highly fragmented.

This can produce variation in definitions, reporting requirements, resources, and data quality across states.

# Part 2. Surveillance Terminology and Design

## Active surveillance

**Definition:** Case reports are actively solicited or cases are directly identified at regular intervals.

Examples may involve:

- Calling sites
- Reviewing records
- Sampling populations
- Systematic testing

### Advantages

- More complete case ascertainment
- More consistent procedures
- Better control over who is included

### Limitations

- Expensive
- Labor intensive
- May still miss people outside the sampled population

---

## Passive surveillance

**Definition:** Health providers report cases to the health department when cases are identified.

### Advantages

- Less expensive
- Can cover large populations
- Works continuously through routine care systems

### Limitations

- Underreporting
- Variation in provider reporting
- Dependence on health-care access and testing
- Delayed reporting

---

# 3. Case Definitions

A surveillance system needs rules defining who counts as a case.

The slides emphasize questions such as:

- Will the definition cause **underreporting** or **overreporting**?
- What definition should be used?
- In which population should it be used?
- Should the definition use clinical criteria, laboratory criteria, or both?

### Example: influenza-like illness

A simple case definition might use:

- Fever
- Cough

But fever and cough can be caused by many pathogens besides influenza.

This means a broad clinical definition may be **sensitive** but not highly **specific**.

# 4. The “Iceberg” Concept

The figure on **page 15** illustrates that the cases visible to the health-care system are only the “tip of the iceberg.”

At the host/person level, infection can range from:

- Classic symptoms / severe disease
- Moderate or mild illness
- Infection without clinical illness (**asymptomatic**)
- Exposure without infection

### Why it matters

A surveillance system based only on diagnosed clinical illness may underestimate true infection burden.

---

# 5. Asymptomatic Carriers

An **asymptomatic carrier**:

- Has the organism
- Has no clinical symptoms
- May, in some cases, have no serologic evidence
- Can still infect others

The epidemiologic importance of a carrier state depends on:

- Infectivity
- Duration of the carrier state

### Historical examples

The lecture mentions:

- **“Typhoid Mary”**
- The historical use of the term **“Patient Zero”** in early U.S. HIV/AIDS investigation, while noting that the true history is more complicated

The key lesson is that asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic transmission can make surveillance much harder.

# Part 3. Major U.S. Infectious-Disease Surveillance Systems

The lecture identifies major categories:

- Reportable / notifiable diseases
- Foodborne illness
- Antimicrobial resistance
- Vaccination uptake
- Vaccine adverse events
- Respiratory viral infections such as influenza and COVID-19

---

# 6. National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System (NNDSS)

The lecture states that more than 120 diseases are nationally notifiable.

Reporting often occurs through **automatic laboratory reporting**.

Notifiable conditions include:

- Rare diseases with major public-health consequences, such as smallpox
- Diseases requiring ongoing intervention, such as hepatitis
- Conditions requiring monitoring, including antimicrobial resistance

---

# 7. Foodborne Disease Surveillance

Multiple systems work together.

## FoodNet

**Foodborne Diseases Active Surveillance Network**

- Uses active surveillance
- Monitors infections commonly transmitted through food

## PulseNet

- Uses molecular surveillance
- Uses pathogen sequencing to identify clusters
- Helps detect infections that may share a common source

### Key idea

Different surveillance methods can be combined: epidemiologic surveillance identifies cases, while molecular surveillance helps link cases to a common outbreak.

---

# 8. Antimicrobial Resistance and Health-Care-Associated Infections

## NARMS

**National Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring System for Enteric Bacteria**

Monitors antimicrobial-resistance patterns across:

- Human infections
- Retail meat
- Food animals

## NHSN

**National Healthcare Safety Network**

Hospitals regularly report information such as:

- Admissions
- Infections
- Health-care-associated infections (HAIs)

The NHSN was also used during COVID-19 to monitor **hospital surge capacity**.

### Risk adjustment

The lecture includes an NHSN example in which patient-level risk factors are used to estimate the probability of surgical-site infection.

Risk adjustment is important because hospitals may serve populations with different baseline risks.

---

# 9. Antibiotic-Resistant Gonorrhea

The **Gonococcal Isolate Surveillance Project (GISP)** monitors antimicrobial susceptibility in gonococcal isolates across the United States.

The historical timeline in the lecture demonstrates how treatment recommendations changed repeatedly as resistance emerged.

### Main lesson

Surveillance is necessary not just to count cases, but to determine whether the tools used to treat disease are still effective.

---

# 10. Vaccination Coverage

Two major U.S. sources are described.

## National Immunization Survey

- Active surveillance
- Uses phone surveys
- Assesses national vaccination patterns

## State registries

- Reporting requirements differ by state
- The lecture notes that Michigan mandates childhood reporting but not adult reporting
- Registries are used in part to support school-based vaccination requirements

### Implication

Different data sources may capture different populations and may not be directly interchangeable.

---

# 11. Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS)

VAERS accepts reports of clinically significant medical events occurring after vaccination, even when the reporter is unsure whether vaccination caused the event.

Reports can come from:

- Health-care providers
- Manufacturers
- State immunization programs
- Vaccine recipients
- Others

### Critical interpretation point

A VAERS report is a **signal**, not proof that a vaccine caused the event.

The lecture shows VAERS feeding into the **Vaccine Safety Datalink**, which is used for follow-up research and safety-signal monitoring.

---

# 12. Vaccine Safety Datalink and Rapid Cycle Analysis

The Vaccine Safety Datalink can monitor outcomes over time and compare observed events with expected rates.

The rapid-cycle workflow shown on **page 32** is:

1. Monitor prespecified outcomes regularly.
2. Compare observed and expected event rates.
3. Identify a potential safety signal if the observed rate is significantly higher.
4. Conduct additional analyses.
5. If a true signal is confirmed, conduct formal epidemiologic investigation.

# Part 4. Using Surveillance to Study Emerging Disease

## Testing evolves over time

The lecture contrasts three stages.

### At emergence

Testing may be:

- Centralized
- Restricted to high-priority cases
- Rationed

Early goals include:

1. Informing clinical care
2. Supporting contact tracing and quarantine

### Days to weeks

- Some clinical testing expands
- Commercial testing grows

### Months to years

Testing becomes more differentiated into:

1. **Clinical testing**  
   Tests people who need clinical attention.

2. **Surveillance testing**  
   Tests a sample of the population to define epidemiology and guide public-health resources.

3. **Mitigation testing**  
   Supports individual and group risk management.

---

# 13. U.S. Flu VE Network

The U.S. Flu Vaccine Effectiveness Network is presented as an example of active surveillance.

It:

- Enrolls people meeting a common case definition such as cough
- Tests participants for influenza and SARS-CoV-2
- Collects vaccine information from self-report, records, and state registries

This provides an example of how an existing surveillance infrastructure can be adapted to study a newly emerging pathogen.

---

# 14. Surveillance Data Can Change Policy

The influenza examples show surveillance results leading to policy changes.

The lecture presents evidence that **live attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV)** performed worse than **inactivated influenza vaccine (IIV)** during the 2015–2016 season, especially in children.

The subsequent slide shows a CDC policy response in which ACIP voted against use of LAIV for the 2016–2017 flu season.

### Lesson

Surveillance data can move from:

**Data collection → Analysis → Evidence → Policy change**

# 15. Detecting Emerging Viruses

The lecture presents examples involving:

- Influenza A(H3N2)
- SARS-CoV-2
- Mortality surveillance
- Emergency-department syndromic surveillance
- Existing influenza vaccine-effectiveness networks

The SARS-CoV-2 examples demonstrate that pre-existing surveillance systems can provide early evidence about when and where a new pathogen is spreading.

# Important Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Surveillance** | Ongoing systematic collection, analysis, interpretation, and use of health data. |
| **Active surveillance** | Cases are actively sought or solicited by the surveillance system. |
| **Passive surveillance** | Providers or laboratories report cases when they encounter them. |
| **Case definition** | Standard criteria used to determine whether a person counts as a case. |
| **Notifiable disease** | A disease that must be reported to public-health authorities under applicable rules. |
| **Asymptomatic carrier** | An infected person without clinical symptoms who may still transmit infection. |
| **Syndromic surveillance** | Monitoring symptom patterns or clinical syndromes that may signal emerging disease. |
| **Risk adjustment** | Statistical adjustment for differences in baseline patient risk when comparing outcomes. |
| **Safety signal** | A pattern suggesting a possible adverse effect that requires further investigation. |

---

# Main Argument / Conclusion

Infectious-disease surveillance is a complex, multi-layered infrastructure rather than a single data source. The usefulness of surveillance data depends on how cases are defined, how people enter the system, which populations are represented, how testing is performed, and how reporting systems are organized.

The lecture’s broader public-health message is that **surveillance systems are essential for detecting disease, evaluating interventions, monitoring safety, and changing policy—but their results must always be interpreted in light of how the data were collected.**

---

# Quick Review

- Active surveillance seeks cases; passive surveillance waits for routine reporting.
- Clinical cases are often only the visible “tip” of the infection iceberg.
- Case definitions strongly affect sensitivity and specificity.
- Asymptomatic transmission complicates surveillance.
- NNDSS, FoodNet, PulseNet, NARMS, NHSN, VAERS, and Vaccine Safety Datalink serve different purposes.
- VAERS identifies signals; it does not establish causation by itself.
- Existing surveillance networks can be repurposed during emerging outbreaks.
- Surveillance data can directly influence public-health policy.
